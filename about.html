How this map knows what’s being built in Spokane

Where the projects come from, how they’re sorted, and what the data can’t tell you. For the numbers themselves, see Stats.

By Anthony Gill, writer of Spokane Rising.

How this map works

Spokane in Progress tracks housing projects of two or more homes inside the City of Spokane, from the first pre-development meeting with the city through completion. It is produced by Anthony Gill, writer of Spokane Rising, as an independent project, not affiliated with or endorsed by the City of Spokane.

Every morning a script reads the city’s public permit records, adds new housing projects, moves existing ones forward when their permits change, and refreshes the map and the stats. Most projects get here that way, so expect the occasional mistake; the limits are spelled out below.

Anthony adds the projects the records miss and fills in what they leave out, such as developers, photos and renderings, and fixes what they get wrong. Those hand edits stick: the daily update can move a project to a later stage, but it never undoes a correction.

What this map can’t tell you

  • It’s not every project. Projects in plan review sometimes appear in the city’s monthly report before its public permit map, and projects named without housing words (“Gardens,” “Haven,” “Tower”) can be missed until a building permit is issued.
  • “Under construction” is an estimate. The city’s public records don’t include inspections, so the map infers construction from trade permits issued after the building permit. A project can sit at “Approved” after work has started, or the reverse.
  • Some projects have no unit count yet. Unit counts come from the city’s weekly permit report when a permit is issued, from permit descriptions, or from the monthly report’s tables of large projects. Projects without a count are drawn as hollow rings and add nothing to the totals, which is why the headline says “at least.”
  • Large-project unit counts can be out of date. The monthly report’s project tables are updated less often than the report itself; the copy used here was last updated May 31, 2026.
  • Dates only go back about three years. The city’s public permit map keeps roughly three years of records, so no project here shows a filing before December 2023. The date a project reached its current stage is when this site first saw it there; dates from before November 2026 are estimates.
  • Building types are inferred. They come from permit wording and unit counts, so a few will be wrong.
  • Finished housing is undercounted. Projects that were already complete when the map first found them are generally left off, so “Complete” is not a count of homes built.
  • Pre-application isn’t a promise. A pre-development conference is a meeting about an idea. Homes at that stage are reported separately as “proposed.”

Why these numbers differ from the city’s

The city counts permits; this map counts projects. A single apartment complex can have several building permits, one per building, and the city’s reports count each. This map groups permits on the same parcel, under the same project name, or on the same street by the same builder into one project, and counts every home in it once. The city’s reports also count by the month a permit was issued, while this map shows where every active project stands today.

How projects are sorted

Stages

Pre-application
The developer has met the city at a pre-development conference to talk through a proposal, but hasn’t filed a building permit. A proposal, not a commitment: many change or never go ahead.
Applied
A building permit application is on file and the city is reviewing the plans.
Approved
The city has approved or issued the building permit. Construction may not have started yet.
Under construction
Work is probably under way: the building permit is issued and trade permits (electrical, plumbing, mechanical) have been issued since. The city’s public records don’t include inspections, so this is an estimate.
Complete
Every building permit in the project has been finaled.
Stalled
Marked by hand when a project has clearly stopped.

Building types and middle housing

Each project gets a building type from its permit descriptions, its name and the number of homes per building: a duplex, triplex or fourplex, fiveplex or sixplex, townhomes, cottage housing, apartments, a house with an accessory dwelling unit (ADU), a conversion or addition to an existing building, or senior and group living.

Middle housing follows Washington’s legal definition: buildings compatible in scale with single-family houses that hold two or more attached, stacked or clustered homes, including duplexes, triplexes, fourplexes, fiveplexes, sixplexes, townhouses, stacked flats, courtyard apartments and cottage housing (RCW 36.70A.030). Accessory dwelling units aren’t on that list, so a house with an ADU is not counted as middle housing here. Townhouses count at any project size.

Neighborhoods and districts

Each project is placed in one of the city’s 29 neighborhood councils and three City Council districts by looking up its map point in the city’s boundary maps. Some projects show a more familiar name in the list, like Kendall Yards or South Perry; the filters always use the official council.

Sources

  • City of Spokane permit records, from the city’s public permit map (permit activity map), read daily: building permits, pre-development conferences and every other permit on the same parcels.
  • City of Spokane weekly permit reports, for exact unit counts when a building permit is issued.
  • The city’s monthly permit report to the City Council, for unit counts on large projects in plan review and early proposals.
  • City of Spokane boundary maps, for neighborhood councils and council districts.
  • City of Spokane zoning map (Spokane GIS), for each project’s zone.
  • Spokane Transit’s published schedules (GTFS), for the distance to frequent transit. A stop counts as frequent when at least one route runs every 15 minutes or better in one direction between 10 a.m. and 2 p.m. on a weekday.
  • Spokane County Assessor parcel records, for site size (homes per acre) and linked from each project (SCOUT).
  • Base map: OpenFreeMap and OpenStreetMap contributors. Satellite imagery: Esri, Maxar, Earthstar Geographics.

Corrections and the data

Spot a project that’s missing, misplaced or out of date? Email spokanerising@gmail.com with the address and what’s wrong, or use “Report a correction” on any project page. Corrections are made by hand and stay in place through the daily updates.

Every project on the map can be downloaded as a spreadsheet from the Stats page.
